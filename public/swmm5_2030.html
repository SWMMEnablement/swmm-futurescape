The United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Storm Water Management Model (SWMM) is a dynamic rainfall–runoff–subsurface runoff simulation model used for single-event to long-term (continuous) simulation of the surface/subsurface hydrology quantity and quality from primarily urban/suburban areas.

It can simulate the rainfall-runoff, runoff, evaporation, infiltration and groundwater connection for roofs, streets, grassed areas, rain gardens and ditches and pipes. The hydrology component of SWMM operates on a collection of subcatchment areas divided into impervious and pervious areas with and without depression storage to predict runoff and pollutant loads. Low impact development (LID) and best management practice areas on the subcatchment can be modeled to reduce runoff. The routing or hydraulics section transports water and water-quality constituents through a system of closed pipes, open channels, storage/treatment devices, ponds, pumps, orifices, weirs, outlets, outfalls and other regulators.

Program description

SWMM is a dynamic rainfall-runoff-routing simulation model used for single event or long-term (continuous) simulation of runoff quantity and quality from primarily urban areas. The runoff component operates on a collection of subcatchments that receive precipitation and generate runoff and pollutant loads. The routing portion transports this runoff through pipes, channels, storage/treatment devices, pumps, and regulators.

SWMM accounts for hydrologic processes including time-varying rainfall; evaporation of standing surface water; snow accumulation and melting; rainfall interception from depression storage; infiltration into unsaturated soil layers; percolation into groundwater layers; interflow between groundwater and the drainage system; nonlinear reservoir routing of overland flow; and capture and retention via LID practices.

History

SWMM was first developed between 1969–1971 and has undergone four major upgrades: Version 2 (1973–75), Version 3 (1979–81), Version 4 (1985–88), and Version 5 (2001–04). The current edition, Version 5.2.4, is a complete re-write in C and runs under Windows and (recompiled) Unix.

SWMM conceptual model

SWMM conceptualizes a drainage system as flows between several environmental compartments: Atmosphere (rain gages), Land Surface (subcatchments and LIDs), Groundwater (aquifers), and Transport (nodes, links, storage).

Model parameters

Simulated parameters include surface roughness, depression storage, slope, and flow path length for subcatchments; Horton max/min rates and decay constant, Green-Ampt hydraulic conductivity, initial moisture deficit and suction head, or NRCS (SCS) curve number for infiltration; Manning's roughness for conduits; and buildup/washoff function coefficients and first-order decay coefficients for water quality.

Hydrology and hydraulics capabilities

SWMM 5 accounts for time-varying rainfall, evaporation, snow accumulation and melting, depression storage interception, infiltration, percolation to groundwater, interflow, and nonlinear reservoir routing of overland flow. Its hydraulic engine routes through pipes, channels, storage units, and regulators, supporting backwater, surcharging, reverse flow, and pressurized flow.

Classical infiltration method (Modified Horton)

Based on empirical observations that infiltration decays exponentially from an initial maximum rate to a minimum rate over a long rainfall event. The Modified Horton variant uses cumulative infiltration in excess of the minimum rate as its state variable, giving a more accurate estimate at low rainfall intensities.

Green–Ampt method

Assumes a sharp wetting front separates saturated soil above from soil at initial moisture content below. Inputs are initial moisture deficit, hydraulic conductivity, and suction head at the wetting front; recovery during dry periods is empirically related to hydraulic conductivity.

Curve number method

Adopted from the NRCS (SCS) curve number method. Total infiltration capacity is derived from the tabulated curve number and depleted as a function of cumulative rainfall and remaining capacity, with a recovery time for fully saturated soils.

Routing options

SWMM supports steady-flow, kinematic wave, and full dynamic wave (Saint-Venant) routing. Dynamic wave routing handles backwater, surcharging, reverse flow, and pressurized flow at the cost of shorter time steps.

Integrated hydrology/hydraulics

A major advance in SWMM 5 is the integration of subsurface flow with hydraulic computation of the drainage network. The engine solves surface runoff, subsurface hydrology, and climate forcing at the hydrologic time step, then routes links and nodes at a (typically smaller) hydraulic time step using interpolated boundary conditions. Versions newer than 5.1.007 allow global monthly adjustments to rainfall, temperature, and evaporation for climate-change studies.

Low-impact development components

LID controls in SWMM 5 include bioretention cells, rain gardens, green roofs, infiltration trenches, permeable pavement, rain barrels/cisterns, and vegetative swales. Each is composed of surface, pavement, soil, storage, drainmat, and drain layers as appropriate.

SWMM5 components

The major SWMM 5 objects are rain gages, subcatchments, nodes (junction, outfall, storage, divider), links (with many closed and open conduit shapes), pollutants, land uses, time patterns, curves, time series, control rules, transects, aquifers, RDII unit hydrographs, snowmelt parameter sets, custom shapes, and LID treatment units.

SWMM 3 and 4 to 5 converter

A bundled converter migrates up to two legacy SWMM 3 / 4 input files (typically Runoff + Transport or Runoff + Extran) into a single SWMM 5 .inp dataset. SWMM 3 / 3.5 files are fixed format; SWMM 4 files are free format; the converter auto-detects the version. Converted pieces can be merged with a text editor.

SWMM-CAT Climate Change Add-On

The Storm Water Management Model Climate Adjustment Tool (SWMM-CAT, 2014) applies location-specific monthly adjustments to air temperature, evaporation, precipitation, and the 24-hour design storm. Adjustments are derived from CMIP3 climate models via EPA's CREAT project and selected by latitude/longitude or ZIP code, time horizon (near- or far-term), and outcome (Hot/Dry, Warm/Wet, or Median).

EPA stormwater calculator based on SWMM5

The EPA National Stormwater Calculator (SWC), released in 2013, is a desktop application built on the SWMM 5 engine that estimates annual rainwater volume and runoff frequency for any US site using national soil, topography, rainfall, and evaporation databases. Users select land cover and LID controls (rain garden, green roof, permeable pavement, cistern, etc.) to compare scenarios.

SWMM platforms

Commercial and open-source platforms built on the SWMM5 engine include PCSWMM (CHI), InfoSWMM (Innovyze), XPSWMM, SewerGEMS (Bentley), MIKE URBAN (DHI), and PySWMM (OWA).

From SWMM 5 to SWMM 7

Following SWMM 5.2.x (2023) and the SWMM 6 “Open Engine” refactor in 2026, SWMM 7 introduced pluggable solver back-ends. The default dynwave-pinn solver retains the canonical Saint-Venant formulation at junctions and conduits, but accelerates the iterative successive- approximation step with a learned preconditioner trained on more than 12 million historical model runs contributed through the EPA SWMM Commons.

Physics-informed neural solvers

PINNs in SWMM 7 are constrained to conserve mass and momentum at every reporting step. Benchmarks against analytical Stoker dam-break and Preissmann-slot pressurization tests show errors below 0.5% while running 8–40× faster than the legacy SWMM 5 dynamic-wave engine on citywide networks (>500k links).

Learned closures

Sub-grid LID, street-gutter, and inlet-capture behavior replaced with calibrated neural surrogates.

Differentiable

The full pipeline is autograd-friendly, enabling gradient-based calibration against gauge data.

Uncertainty

Ensemble heads emit per-link flow PDFs, not point estimates — finally usable for risk-based design.

Generative rainfall & climate forcing

SWMM-CAT, the 2014 climate add-on, was superseded in 2028 by SWMM-Gen, a diffusion model trained on CMIP7 ensembles and on 40 years of NEXRAD MRMS radar. SWMM-Gen produces spatially coherent, sub-kilometer storm fields conditioned on a chosen SSP scenario and return period. Users can prompt: "100-yr 3-hr convective storm, SSP3-7.0, Phoenix 2055" and receive a 250 m × 250 m × 5 min hyetograph series ready for ingestion.

Autocalibration and inverse modeling

SWMM 7's swmm calibrate command takes a list of telemetry streams (flow, depth, rainfall, water-quality) and returns posterior distributions over Manning's n, depression storage, Horton parameters, and per-subcatchment imperviousness. Variational inference replaces the manual nudging that dominated practice through the 2010s.

Natural-language model authoring

"Add a 0.4-ha bioretention cell at junction J-217, draining 60% of the adjacent parking lot, with 18 in of engineered soil and an underdrain at 12 in." → SWMM generates the LID Control, modifies the subcatchment routing, and re-runs the design storm.

Reception and controversy

Adoption has been brisk in research circles and at consultancies but slower at regulators. FEMA conditionally approved SWMM 7 for NFIP modeling in 2029 only when run in physics-strict mode (PINN closures disabled). Critics argue that learned closures obscure the auditable lineage that public-sector models require; proponents counter that the weights, training data, and provenance manifests are all in the public domain.

The 2026–2029 regulatory pivot

The EPA's 2027 revision of the NPDES MS4 General Permit explicitly references SWMM 6+ as the reference implementation for compliance modeling. The EU's revised Urban Wastewater Treatment Directive (2028) requires combined-sewer-overflow assessments to be reproducible with an open-source engine — in practice, SWMM.

Forward-looking IDF curves

Static intensity-duration-frequency curves were retired from EPA guidance in 2028 in favor of climate-adjusted IDF ensembles. SWMM projects now ship with an [ADAPTATION] section pointing to a CMIP7-derived rainfall library indexed by SSP scenario, decade, and downscaled grid cell.

Compound flood coupling

SWMM 6.3 introduced bidirectional coupling with the SCHISM and ADCIRC coastal models via the open OASIS3-MCT coupler. A junction with a coastal boundary now receives time-varying tide, surge, and wave setup, allowing genuine compound-flood (pluvial + fluvial + coastal) simulation inside a single SWMM run. This was the headline feature behind FEMA's 2029 Risk Rating 3.0 updates.

Architecture of a SWMM digital twin

A canonical SWMM digital twin has four layers: the physical network of pipes, gauges, and actuators; an edge tier that timestamps and buffers telemetry; a twin core running an ensemble of SWMM instances (typically 64 members); and a decision layer surfacing dashboards and pushing setpoints back to SCADA.

Sensor classes & ingestion

  • Level sensors — ultrasonic and radar in manholes; 1 min cadence.
  • Flow meters — area-velocity, tracer-dilution; 5 min cadence.
  • Inlet cameras — CV models infer clogging fraction.
  • X-band radar & mesonets — 250 m hyetograph nowcasts.
  • Quality probes — turbidity, conductivity, fluorescent dissolved organic matter.
  • Citizen reports — geotagged street-flood images via the EPA OpenFlood API.

Data assimilation

SWMM-Live ships with an Ensemble Kalman Filter that updates depths at observed nodes every 15 seconds and propagates corrections upstream and downstream via the model's Jacobian. For large networks, a localized EnKF with 5 km radius keeps the update tractable.

Real-time control (RTC) with reinforcement learning

In a 2029 South Bend pilot, RL-controlled inflatable dams and pump setpoints reduced combined-sewer overflow volume by 71% over a rule-based RTC baseline, using only existing actuators.

RL agents are trained inside a SWMM-Gym environment, then deployed with a hard safety envelope: a model-predictive-control supervisor blocks any action whose simulated outcome exceeds surcharge or overflow thresholds.

Forecasting workflow

  1. X-band radar nowcast → SWMM-Gen ensemble (32 members, 0–6 h).
  2. Each member drives a SWMM-Live instance.
  3. Probabilistic flood maps published every 5 minutes.
  4. Alerts pushed to Wireless Emergency Alerts and city dashboards.

From INP files to SWMM-JSON

The legacy fixed/free-format .inp text file — beloved and reviled in equal measure since 1971 — was formally deprecated in 2029 in favor of SWMM-JSON 1.0, an Open Geospatial Consortium standard. SWMM-JSON adds explicit units, CRS metadata, sensor links, provenance, and a manifest of all data sources. Round-trip conversion from .inp is bundled in every SWMM 7 distribution.

The SWMM Commons

The SWMM Commons, launched in 2026, hosts roughly 48,000 publicly shared drainage network models from utilities, universities, and consultants worldwide. Each is citable via DOI and tagged with land use, climate zone, and demographic context. The Commons underpinned the training data for SWMM 7's neural closures and is, by EPA mandate, mirrored on Zenodo and IPFS to survive registry outages.

Reproducible runs

Every uploaded run carries a hash of engine version, inputs, and forcing — re-running anywhere reproduces results bit-for-bit (in physics-strict mode).

FAIR data

Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable. The Commons API speaks STAC and OGC API-Features natively.

Equity tags

Models can be filtered by EJ score, climate zone, or income decile to surface chronically under-modeled communities.

Reproducibility & provenance

SWMM 7 emits a W3C PROV-O document alongside every run. Disputes over flood-disclosure modeling — common in litigation through the late 2020s — increasingly hinge on this provenance trail rather than on expert testimony alone.

Browser-native SWMM

The engine was compiled to WebAssembly in 2025 (swmm-wasm) and to WebGPU in 2028. A full 50,000-node citywide simulation now runs in a tab — no install, no Windows VM. Educational uptake has been dramatic: by 2030, more than 180 universities use the browser build as their primary teaching platform.

Capacity building in the Global South

UNESCO-IHE's SWMM Academy, the World Bank's City Resilience Program, and the African Water Association co-fund a free certification track in English, French, Portuguese, Spanish, Arabic, Mandarin, Swahili, and Bahasa Indonesia. By 2030, over 62,000 practitioners hold a SWMM certification, more than triple the 2024 total, with the fastest growth in West Africa and Southeast Asia.

Critiques & open problems

Critics note that openness does not automatically translate into equity: the most data-rich models still concentrate in wealthy cities, and learned closures inherit those biases. Ongoing work focuses on (i) sparse-data calibration techniques, (ii) culturally appropriate default LID libraries, and (iii) co-design protocols that include community knowledge as first-class model inputs.
